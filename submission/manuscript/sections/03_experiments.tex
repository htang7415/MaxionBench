\subsection{Artifact audit}

The paper is built from the archived bundle \texttt{results/20260429T033427Z}. The archive contains the run artifacts, generated figures, \hotpotmaxionbench{} manifest and checksums, conformance matrix, and operator command documents. A strict-schema validation pass over the archived MaxionBench runs checked 72 run directories and returned zero errors. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:portable-support} reports passing conformance and behavior-card coverage for FAISS CPU, LanceDB in-process, pgvector, and Qdrant; LanceDB service is excluded because it has no conformance row.

The artifact is intended to make each paper claim traceable. It contains the benchmark code, archived run bundle, validation commands, conformance matrix, generated tables and figures, query-level paired-audit summaries, checksums, \hotpotmaxionbench{} Croissant-style metadata \citep{mlcommons2024croissant}, and a claim-evidence map. All main results below should therefore be read as local single-node deployment evidence under the recorded configs, not as universal engine rankings.

\subsection{Decision audit results}

We organize results around four deployment-audit questions rather than a single leaderboard ranking. First, under the default 200 ms \maxrowp policy, FAISS CPU with bge-small is the policy-selected strict-latency configuration for all three workloads. Second, this decision is objective-sensitive: removing the p99 cap or optimizing quality changes selected configurations. Third, paired audits separate clear margins from aggregate near-ties. Fourth, budget stability is workload-dependent, so short screening runs cannot always replace B2 decisions.

Table~\ref{tab:portable-main-results} is a strict-latency deployment-card summary. Under the 200 ms \maxrowp threshold, all three workloads select FAISS CPU with \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5}. The FAISS rows are exact FlatIP local-baseline rows, while service engines include their adapter and service overhead; the table therefore supports a local deployment decision, not a distributed product ranking. The S1 selected row reaches \ndcg{} 0.506 with a 95\% confidence interval of 0.494--0.516 and \maxrowp 11.2 ms. S2 selects the same engine and embedding, with \ndcg{} 0.506, \postinsertfive{} 0.830, and \maxrowp 10.0 ms. S3 selects FAISS CPU with \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} at \ecov{} 0.852 and \maxrowp 22.8 ms.

S2 should be interpreted as agent-facing post-insert top-10 retrievability, not low-level index visibility. S1 and the static-retrieval component of S2 use the same 894-query BEIR-format evaluation pool under the 50K-document cap, which is why their static \ndcg{} and packed-token summaries can match. S2 adds CRAG-500 write/retrieve probes after the adapter insert-plus-flush path returns. Across two B2 strict-choice repeats with 500 events each, 830 of 1{,}000 post-insert observations are retrieved by the 1s probe; no additional observations recover by 5s, leaving 170 of 1{,}000 absent at the 5s probe. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:s2-postinsert}, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:s2-write-diagnostics}, and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:s2-post-insert-examples} report the fixed-probe diagnostics and example event/query/document IDs.

The benchmark-design ablation is reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decision-error-ablation}. Its main implication is that removing any audit component changes the interpretation: the p99 gate separates strict-latency decisions from cost-only/no-p99 choices, objective separation prevents quality-first rows from being conflated with strict-latency rows, budget reporting exposes unstable S1/S2 screening runs, paired audits prevent aggregate near-ties from being overclaimed, and conformance filtering keeps unsupported service rows out of paper-facing tables.

Table~\ref{tab:portable-decision-table} and Figure~\ref{fig:decision-surface} show objective sensitivity directly. The strict-latency and cost-only/no-p99 objectives agree for S1 and S2, but S3 shifts to LanceDB in-process with \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} when the p99 cap is removed. Quality-first decisions differ more strongly: LanceDB in-process with \texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5} leads S1, FAISS CPU with \texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5} leads S2, and the archived aggregate rule selects pgvector with \texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5} for S3. The point is not that any one 200 ms threshold is universal; the point is that \sys makes the policy explicit and auditable.

The S3 quality-first margin is the highest-risk aggregate claim, so we audit it with matched queries. Using all B2 rows, pgvector-base exceeds FAISS-base by only 0.00016 \ecov{} with an aggregate-row bootstrap interval of -0.00280 to 0.00304. The matched audit in Figure~\ref{fig:s3-paired-audit} removes even that apparent edge: pgvector IVF32 and IVF64 are each 0.0001 \ecov{} below FAISS exact FlatIP on the 5{,}000-query matched-audit subset, with paired intervals including zero. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:s3-paired-quality} gives the numeric rows, and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:s3-all-evidence-hit} records the stricter binary all\_evidence\_hit@10 audit.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:strict-decision-margins} makes the strict-latency decision margins explicit. For S1 and S2, FAISS CPU with \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} is not a relevance-quality win over the closest LanceDB in-process candidate; it is a cost and quality tie that the deployment rule resolves by lower \maxrowp and higher throughput. For S3, the nearest strict-cost alternative is not a tie: Qdrant with \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} is more expensive and lower quality, even though its \maxrowp is 4.6 ms lower in the repeat-aware table. The highest-quality strict-latency S3 option remains FAISS CPU with \texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5}, which improves \ecov{} by 0.020 over \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} but increases the normalized context-cost proxy by 0.704 and \maxrowp by 33.4 ms on the B2 audit rows.

The normalized context-cost proxy is stable under the coefficient sensitivity tested in the artifact. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:cost-sensitivity} rescales only \(c_{\mathrm{llm\_in}}\) from 0.1$\times$ to 10$\times$ and leaves all strict choices unchanged: S1/S2 have identical retrieved-token counts for the strict choice and nearest strict-cost competitor, while S3 FAISS/bge-small retrieves slightly fewer tokens than Qdrant/bge-small. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:latency-distribution} reports p50, p95, mean p99, and the min--max p99 range over archived B2 run rows; these are conditional on the fixed local run and should not be read as population-level latency intervals.

The budget analysis qualifies how much evidence is enough. B0-to-B2 top-1 agreement is zero for S1 and S2, with Spearman correlations 0.79 and 0.43, respectively. S3 has top-1 agreement of one but Spearman correlation 0.31, meaning that the selected strict-latency row is stable while lower-ranked alternatives remain noisy. The benchmark can therefore produce actionable local decisions, but the decision card must include the budget level and stability statistics used to reach them.

\subsection{Targeted replication checks}

Targeted same-machine reruns support the decision-level conclusions but not exact tail-latency reproduction. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:strict-faiss-repeat-audit} shows that the strict FAISS CPU/bge-small choices reproduce the relevant quality values and remain under the 200 ms policy; the S3 bge-base rerun and Figure~\ref{fig:s3-paired-audit} support the objective-sensitivity story; and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:s2-competitor-check} reports a larger FAISS-versus-Qdrant S2 paired check with no hidden Qdrant quality or post-insert retrievability win. Exact repeat numbers remain in the appendix because p99 is local-run evidence rather than a hardware-population interval.
